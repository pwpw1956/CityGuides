How CityGuides works

  1. Choose a city, available time, walking pace and planning style on Home.
  2. At the place where you want the tour to begin, press I AM HERE. Your phone's GPS position becomes the route origin and Home location.
  3. The guide chooses a set of sights that should fit the available time. The first walking leg begins at your recorded GPS position; the selected stops are then ordered into the proposed tour.
  4. Use Map for the pedestrian route and site pins. Selected stops are numbered in route order. Use Previous and Next to move the displayed guide section through today's selected stops.
  5. The pin whose description is currently displayed on Detail has a bright yellow outline. Moving Previous/Next moves that highlight.
  6. With Auto-follow ON, GPS can change the displayed guide section when you enter the defined zone for a site. With it OFF, you control the sections manually.
  7. Re-plan recalculates the proposed selection/order from your current position. The app reports whether the plan changed or no change was needed.
  8. Finish tour can favour a central finish, have no fixed endpoint, return to Sight 1, or Return to Home. Return to Home includes the actual final walking leg and automatically uses no separate return-time reserve. A chequered flag shows the endpoint.
  9. After a detailed route is calculated, CityGuides checks the order in which the route actually encounters the selected sights. If it passes a later sight first, the numbering is corrected and the route is recalculated.
  10. For linear sights, a city guide can state a preferred walking direction. Lewes currently prefers Keere Street downhill unless reaching the preferred end would add an unreasonable approach detour.
  11. Reverse route lets you try the same selected sights in the opposite direction. This manual choice temporarily overrides normal preferred-direction logic for linear sights.
  12. Some very close sights can share an internal cluster. They remain separate numbered sights, but once one cluster member is selected the others become cheaper to add, and the planner prefers to keep selected members together.
  13. On the Map page, every candidate sight is shown both as a coloured pin and in the list below the map. Add/Remove works in either view; both views update immediately. Your explicit choices are retained when you press Re-plan.
  14. Red/unavailable sights cannot be added to today's tour. Blue sights are selected and numbered; green/orange sights remain available alternatives.
  15. Automatic re-planning is OFF by default. If switched on, it is checked after a visited stop and only prompts when the estimated overrun is material — at least 15 minutes or about 15% of remaining time.
  16. Pedestrian routing strongly favours mapped footways and sidewalks and penalises mapped steps, private access and driveways. It can only use barriers, steps and pavements that exist in the underlying OpenStreetMap data.
  17. Direction arrows now come primarily from Valhalla's turn-by-turn manoeuvres, with extra departure arrows after sights and continuation arrows roughly every 200 metres on long stretches. Tap an arrow to see its instruction.
  18. With Auto-follow ON, a small Next-direction strip shows the next Valhalla instruction and approximate distance. Very short route spurs which leave and rejoin the same place without a sight are flagged for diagnosis.
  19. Visited selected sites can be shown grey. GET ME HOME remains disabled until a tour is active and requires confirmation before routing back to the recorded Home position.

Audio controls

Playback speed changes take effect from the next spoken section. Pause/Resume is designed to be reliable across Android and iPhone browsers; resuming restarts the current paragraph rather than risking a browser speech-resume failure. Follow audio gently tracks the written text; manually scrolling the text turns that following off.

How the walking route is chosen

CityGuides now separates the place you are interested in from the point a walker should actually use. Buildings and museums can have a public entrance/approach point; gardens and larger sites can use a practical entrance with a broader GPS zone; streets such as Keere Street can define a short sequence that the route must actually traverse. The planner first asks the pedestrian network for walking times between candidate sights, then balances those times against interest, available time and a penalty for sharp reversals/backtracking. The detailed route is then requested through the chosen access/traversal points.

Why some places are not selected

At present, orange means a place was left out because time, walking involved or priority made another combination preferable. Green marks a useful optional stop. Red means unavailable; blue means selected.
